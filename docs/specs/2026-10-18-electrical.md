# Electrical design (step 3)

*Started 2026-10-18 with the electrical talk (step 3a). Like everything else here, it will change once real numbers show up.*

## What this step has to produce

From the [roadmap](../roadmap.md): no custom PCB, so everything is off-the-shelf boards and breakouts. A system schematic in KiCad and harness diagrams from code (WireViz); a pin map for the flight controller and the Teensy; the power design (the battery connector, the ESC, the 5 V supplies, the current sensor, fuses, wire gauges and connector ratings); checks (voltage drop from the CAD's wire lengths, peak and continuous currents in every setup, rough heat, the power budget for every module combination); and an ngspice simulation showing the safety monitor's switch passes bidirectional DShot, including with the Teensy unpowered.

**Done when:** every connection is specified, and every current, voltage and temperature is inside its rating with margin.

## Settled in the electrical talk

Eight questions, one at a time: where the design lives, the flight controller board, the module bus, the main power path, the 5 V rail, the motor cut circuit, the check limits, and watching it. Power started as one question and split in two once the currents were in.

**Where the design lives (2026-10-18): one connection list, in code, that generates everything.** `design/electrical.yaml` lists every board and its pins, every connector, every wire with its gauge, and which wire route in `design/cad.yaml` it follows. From that one file the code generates the WireViz harness diagrams, the pin map tables, every check (currents, voltage drop, heat, the power budget), a KiCad schematic (each board a block with its pins, joined by net labels, so KiCad's electrical rules check can run on it), and the wires colored by current in the CAD's 3D view. **Why:** it's the CAD's approach again. One place to change a connection, everything regenerates, it can be tested, and the diffs are readable. A schematic of blocks and net labels looks plainer than a hand-drawn one, and that's the cost.

**The flight controller (2026-10-18): the ARK FPV, replacing the Kakute H7.** The board has to carry the receiver, the GPS, the companion computer and the Teensy on full serial ports, the module bus, the lidar, and four bidirectional DShot outputs. The Kakute H7 V1.5 exposes four full serial ports, and PX4 already uses all of them, so the Teensy link would mean taking over the debug console; it has no CAN at all, and its SWD is bare test points. The ARK FPV has five full serial ports (one is PX4's console) plus two receive-only ones, one CAN port, bidirectional DShot on all motor outputs under PX4, 5 V and 12 V supplies, a 3S to 12S input, and a JST-SH debug plug for SWD. It ships with PX4, from a company that develops it. **Why:** it fits every link with room left over, keeps DroneCAN open for the modules, and the debug plug will matter when my own firmware goes on. **Cost:** $195 against $85, it's on backorder (nothing gets bought by default, so that matters less here), and the cable to the Tekko ESC needs re-pinning, because the ESC plugs' pin orders differ. Swapping it into the catalog and the CAD is a task in the electrical build.

**The module bus (2026-10-18): DroneCAN for the modules' parts, plus one serial line for the companion computer.** The module connector is an XT30 for 5 V power (rated 20 A, so the Pi's 5 A is easy) and one JST-GH plug carrying CAN and one serial line. Each module announces itself on CAN, so the firmware knows what's fitted without extra pins. The chute's release servo and the claws' servos sit on small off-the-shelf DroneCAN servo nodes, the replacement for the end-of-life GPS is a DroneCAN GPS, and the Pi talks MAVLink to the flight controller on the serial line, the standard way PX4 talks to a companion computer. **Why:** DroneCAN is the industry standard for drone add-ons, it's one four-wire bus whatever modules are on, and the ARK FPV's CAN port is there for it. **Cost:** P and C each carry a small CAN node, a few grams each, and my own firmware in step 5 has to speak DroneCAN (libcanard, under the MIT license, exists for that). The safety monitor's path to the parachute must not depend on the bus; that's the motor cut and chute circuit question.

**The main power path (2026-10-18): the frame's XT60, no main fuse, small fuses on the side branches.** The sizing model's currents set this one:

| Setup | Hovering | Cruising at 10 m/s | Flat out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base | 9 to 11 A | 7 to 8 A | 90 A (capped from 127 A) |
| Mission | 14 to 17 A | 11 to 14 A | 90 A |
| Max | 16 to 20 A | 13 to 16 A | 90 A |

The main lead carries under 20 A for nearly the whole flight, and 90 A only in short punch-outs. (Corrected later the same day, in the build: this table is the nominal power model, from a full pack to the reserve. The pessimistic model at the reserve, which the checks use, gives up to 23 A in Max with the new parts. The XT60 still runs at two thirds of its rating.) Amass rates the XT60 at 35 A continuous, the XT90 at 45 A and the AS150U at 75 A, so no connector here is rated for 90 A continuous, and none needs to be. The checks judge the XT60 on its real duty: the continuous current against its 35 A rating, plus a heat check on the bursts, whose length the check limits question sets. The branches that tap the battery (the 5 V supply's feed and the flight controller's battery feed) each get a small blade fuse, sized by the checks. The pack gets a fuse link on each cell, the way e-bike packs are built: each one carries its cell's share of the capped current (45 A) and blows on a dead short, which is hundreds of amps. Whether that gap is wide enough for a real fuse link is something the checks have to show. **Why:** the weight margin is gone (Max is 7 g under its limit), the firmware already caps the current, and a fuse in the 90 A line is one more way to drop a healthy drone, the same reasoning that keeps the safety monitor off the main power. **Cost:** without the cell links, nothing would stop a dead short in the main leads, from a wire crushed in a crash, say, and that's a fire risk with Li-ion. A main fuse rated well above the cap would have added 10 to 15 g and another joint in the main line, and the XT90 would have put Max over 2500 g.

*Changed in the build (2026-10-18): the cell links are out.* The fuse check found that no link gauge works at these currents. A dead short at the far end of the battery leads pulls about 480 A (the pack's own resistance limits it to about five times the cap), and the 12 AWG leads' insulation reaches 200 °C after 1.1 s of it. An 18 AWG link opens in 0.95 s, but at the 90 A cap its solder joint passes 163 °C (183 °C less the margin) after 6.4 s; thicker links survive the cap but open too slowly to save the leads. A main fuse big enough never to trip at 90 A is slower still: Littelfuse's MIDI curve gives 3 to 100 s at three times its rating. So there's no electrical short protection on the main leads. They stay short, sleeved and routed away from crush points, and a dead short in them goes on the unproven list.

**The 5 V rail (2026-10-18): one module supply for everything, with checks protecting the Pi.** The Matek BEC12S-PRO (5.2 V, 5 A continuous, 9 A peak) feeds the Pi and the module connector, servo power included. The worry from step 2, that the BEC is rated at exactly the 5 A the Pi 5 asks for, turned out to be mostly about USB: the Pi asks for 5 A so it can pass 1.6 A on to USB devices, and on anything less it limits USB to 600 mA. On its own it measured 9.8 W flat out, under 2 A, and nothing in V is on USB. With the catalog's figures, the BEC carries:

| Setup | Typical | Worst case (every servo stalled) |
|---|---|---|
| Base | 0.1 A (the Teensy) | 0.1 A |
| Base + V | 2.0 A | 2.4 A |
| Mission (V + P) | 2.1 A | 2.8 A |
| Max (V + P + C) | 2.5 A | 5.6 A |

Only Max's worst case goes past 5 A, all of it from the placeholder claws (two standard servos assumed to stall at 1.4 A each). The real risk is a stalling servo pulling the rail down and resetting the Pi, whose undervoltage warning trips at about 4.63 V. So each servo node gets a small capacitor, the checks hold every combination to the BEC's ratings and keep the Pi's voltage up with every servo stalled, and if the real claws fail those checks when they're designed, they bring their own supply.

The rest of the 5 V picture: the flight controller, receiver, GPS and lidar run on the ARK FPV's own 5 V, under half an amp together (ARK doesn't publish that supply's rating, so finding it is a to-do). The Remote ID module has its own battery. The Teensy takes power from both supplies through a pair of diodes, because it has to be able to fire the chute when the flight controller has lost power. No servo runs from the flight controller's 5 V, where a stall could brown it out. The one exception is the chute's release servo as a backup, below. **Why:** it costs no weight, which the 7 g margin can't spare, and every combination passes on typical draw. **Cost:** a stalled servo can still reset the Pi. Annoying, not dangerous: the companion computer going quiet is already a failsafe, and the flight controller never depends on it. A second BEC just for servos would have kept the Pi's rail clean for about 7 to 9 g, putting Max at or over 2500 g, and Matek's two-output UBEC DUO weighs 32 g and tops out at 26 V in.

**The motor cut and chute circuit (2026-10-18): the safety monitor takes over the ESC lines and sends stop.** One fact changed the plan here. AM32, the ESC's firmware, keeps the motors at their last throttle for half a second after DShot frames stop arriving, then shuts them off (its source says "half second timeout when armed"). So the old plan, a switch that just disconnects the four signal lines, would leave the props at the last throttle for half a second after I hit the kill switch.

- **The motor cut:** a four-channel FET multiplexer sits in the four DShot lines between the flight controller and the ESC. It's powered from the flight controller's side, and its select line is pulled toward the flight controller, so with the Teensy off or unpowered the flight controller drives the ESC as if the monitor weren't there. To cut, the Teensy flips the select and sends DShot stop frames itself, in the same bidirectional DShot the flight controller uses, so the motors stop within a millisecond instead of half a second. Its DShot code can only ever send stop, never thrust. Whether a cut can be undone in the air is a firmware question for step 5.
- **Listening:** the Teensy listens to the receiver's CRSF line (the kill switch reaches it there, separately from the flight controller) and to the four DShot lines on the flight controller's side, so it sees what the flight controller is commanding, including when it goes quiet. Every signal into the Teensy goes through a buffer that goes high-impedance when it's unpowered, so a dead Teensy can't drag a line down or get half-powered through it. The same goes for its serial link to the flight controller.
- **The chute:** the flight controller fires it over DroneCAN, through the servo node in P. The Teensy fires it without the bus: two wires of its own through the module connector (a takeover line and a servo signal) reach a one-channel multiplexer in P, in front of the release servo. The takeover line is pulled down, so the CAN node drives the servo unless the Teensy takes over. The module connector's JST-GH gets those two pins. The release servo runs from the module 5 V, with a backup feed from the CAN bus's 5 V through a pair of diodes, so a dead BEC can't disarm the chute. With the BEC at 5.2 V, the BEC side carries the load unless it fails. *Changed in step 3d (2026-10-19): the pair of diodes is an ideal-diode OR (TI's LM66200 on Adafruit's breakout), because the Schottkys' drop left the servo under its range.*
- **The order:** whichever board fires the chute cuts the motors first, then waits a short delay so the props slow down. The delay is a setting in both firmwares, and its starting value comes from the sim in step 6.
- **The circuit sim (ngspice):** DShot600 through the multiplexer with the Teensy powered, unpowered and cutting; the ESC's RPM reply coming back through it; the CRSF and DShot lines with the Teensy off, to show the buffers don't load them; and the takeover, where the ESC should see at most one broken frame, which fails its checksum and gets ignored. Where makers don't publish SPICE models, the parts get modeled from their datasheet figures (on-resistance, capacitance, leakage when unpowered), and the report says so.

**Why:** it stops the motors fast, it fails in the useful direction (an unpowered Teensy changes nothing), and the chute's path from the Teensy never touches the bus. **Cost:** more parts than a plain switch (the multiplexer, buffers, diodes, and the one-channel multiplexer in P), probably a gram or two over the 5 g estimate, to be weighed in the build. The Teensy now has to generate DShot, and AM32 has to accept frames from a second source mid-flight, which the sim and, if it's ever built, the bench have to show.

**The check numbers (2026-10-18): ratings with margin, judged on real duty.** These are my engineering estimates, not taken from a standard (apart from the 80% rule, which electricians use for continuous loads too), and they live in one settings file.

- **Continuous current:** every wire, connector, fuse and supply runs at no more than 80% of its continuous rating, at the highest steady current it sees in any setup. For the main path that's Max's pessimistic hover at the reserve, 23 A with the new parts, against the XT60's 35 A (28 A at 80%).
- **Bursts:** a 10 s burst at the 90 A cap, starting from the hottest steady state. That covers a long full-throttle climb, and punch-outs are shorter. Wires and connectors are judged on how hot they get, not on their continuous rating.
- **Temperature:** every part stays at least 20 °C under its rated maximum, with the air around it at 40 °C (a hot day, inside the frame).
- **Voltage drop:** the main power path loses no more than 2% of the pack's nominal 21.6 V at the cap (0.43 V). Every 5 V load stays inside its published input range with 0.1 V to spare at both ends, and the Pi stays above 4.75 V, worst case included.
- **Supplies:** typical draw at no more than 80% of the continuous rating, and the worst case (every servo stalled) under the peak rating.
- **Fuses:** each one holds 125% of its branch's highest steady current (the 80% rule from the other side) and blows on a short before the wire it protects overheats. The cell links hold their 45 A through the burst and melt on a dead short in under a second, judged with Onderdonk's equation, the standard estimate for how fast a wire fuses.
- **Signals (from the ngspice runs):** rise and fall times under 10% of a bit, every level clears the receiving chip's logic thresholds by 10% of its supply, and every DShot frame decodes to what went in.
- **Unknown ratings:** a part with no published rating gets a conservative assumption, marked as an estimate in the catalog, and the report lists every check that rests on one, so none hides.

**Watching it (2026-10-18): Rerun, plus post-ready stills on every run.**

- **In Rerun:** the drone from the CAD with every wire colored by its current (turbo, the style's colormap for anything continuous), stepping through each module combination and flight phase (hover, cruise, the 90 A burst), with anything past its limit in magenta. The heat check plays as temperature curves through the burst, and the ngspice runs play as scope traces: DShot through the switch with the Teensy on, off and cutting. Each plot opens on the whole run.
- **Stills:** the power tree, the harness diagrams and the schematic on the project's black, and the scope traces of the switch, at the post sizes.
- **Why:** it's the CAD's setup again, one viewer for the whole project that runs in the browser pane. And the trace of DShot passing cleanly with the Teensy unpowered is the one picture that proves the safety design works.

## Settled in the plan

**The GPS (2026-10-18): the ARK GPS on DroneCAN.** The parts research found no DroneCAN GPS with a compass under 16 g that's still sold, 8 g more than the end-of-life M10Q. ARK's GPS (u-blox NEO-M9N, BMM150 compass, a barometer) ties for lightest, PX4 documents it, and its terminator is a software setting. With it, the servo nodes and the rest of the new parts, Mission comes to about 2309 g and still passes, and Max to about 2514 g, over its 2.5 kg limit. ARK's SAM GPS MINI on a serial port weighs 8 g, but Max would still land around 2506 g, and its six wires would have pushed the module connector from 8 pins to 12. Max only has to be reported, and step 3d gives it its real number. The [plan](../plans/2026-10-18-electrical.md) has the rest of the research.
